Google Search Ranking & Discoverability Capstone

Author: Sulamith Singh
Track: Machine Learning

Abstract

This project asks which content pages should be prioritized for refresh to improve future search performance. It uses the anonymized FlyRank content-refresh dataset and evaluates a Random Forest ranking workflow against a transparent baseline. The target is is_declining_label = (trend_direction == "down"), and evaluation uses a client-grouped holdout so clients are not shared between training and testing. On the capstone evaluation, the baseline achieved Precision@50 of 0.24 while the Random Forest achieved 0.74, an improvement of 0.50 (50 percentage points). The result supports historical, directional decision support for human review, but it does not prove that a refresh will cause better Google rankings, traffic, clicks, or visibility.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

Content and SEO teams need a practical way to decide which pages deserve attention first. Reviewing every page equally can use substantial time and effort, so this project focuses on building a ranked review queue from measurable content and search-performance signals.

Research Question

Which content pages should be prioritized for refresh to improve future search performance?

Decision Supported

The analysis supports the decision of which content pages should be refreshed first, allowing content and SEO resources to focus on higher-priority opportunities.

Action

Pages identified as higher-priority opportunities can be reviewed and considered for content refresh before lower-priority pages.

Cost of a Wrong Decision

Refreshing the wrong pages can waste time and effort, while higher-potential pages may continue to lose search visibility or performance.

Honest Framing

This is a decision-support workflow based on historical search and content-performance patterns. It identifies pages that may be useful candidates for review; it does not guarantee that refreshing a page will improve rankings or traffic.

3. Methodology

3.1 Modeling Task

The task is treated as a ranking/prioritization problem: identify pages that match the declining label and place stronger candidates earlier in a human-review queue.

3.2 Model

A Random Forest model is compared with a transparent hand-written baseline. The learned model is evaluated on the same ranking metric and validation design as the baseline.

3.3 Baseline

The baseline is a simple, transparent refresh-prioritization rule. Its purpose is to provide an understandable benchmark against which the learned ranking can be compared.

3.4 Primary Metric

Precision@50 is the primary metric. It measures the proportion of the 50 highest-ranked pages that are actually labelled as declining.

3.5 Validation Design

The final capstone evaluation uses a client-grouped holdout. Approximately 20% of clients are held out for testing, and pages from those clients are not placed in the training set. This is more realistic than randomly splitting individual pages when the goal is to assess performance on unseen clients.

3.6 Leakage Control

The target is derived from trend_direction. Therefore, outcome-revealing fields such as trend_direction and trend_pct are not used as predictive features. This prevents directly leaking the target into the model inputs.

Interpretation rule: The model is a prioritization aid for historical patterns. A strong Precision@50 result does not establish that a recommended content refresh will cause a future ranking or traffic improvement.

The Random Forest achieved a Precision@50 of 0.74, compared with 0.24 for the baseline. The difference is 0.50, or 50 percentage points.

In practical terms, among the 50 pages ranked highest for review, the model identified substantially more pages matching the declining-label definition than the baseline under the client-holdout evaluation.

Honest Interpretation

The result shows that the Random Forest produced a stronger ranking of historically declining pages than the simple baseline on the client-holdout evaluation. It should be interpreted as historical, directional decision support rather than proof of Google's ranking algorithm or proof that a content refresh will improve search performance.

5. Limitations and Honest Framing

  1. Historical data: The model learns from patterns in the released dataset. Future search behavior may differ.
  2. No causal claim: A high-priority score does not mean that refreshing a page will cause higher rankings, traffic, clicks, or visibility.
  3. Client holdout: The grouped validation is a stronger generalization test for unseen clients, but it does not guarantee performance on every future client or dataset.
  4. Label limitation: The declining label represents an observed trend direction; it does not directly measure whether a page truly needs a content refresh.
  5. Feature limitations: Important factors not present in the released dataset may also affect search performance.
  6. Human review: Model rankings should be treated as a prioritized review queue, not an automatic content-change system.

6. Ranked Recommendations / Action Playbook

The model output should be used to determine where to look first. The final content decision remains with a human reviewer.

Priority 1 — Review the highest-ranked pages first

Start with pages receiving the strongest model-based priority. These pages form the first part of the human-review queue.

Priority 2 — Check freshness and relevance

Review whether the content is still accurate, useful, complete, and relevant to the page's intended search need.

Priority 3 — Review search-performance signals

Examine clicks, impressions, CTR, average position, and other available signals to understand the observed performance pattern before changing content.

Priority 4 — Choose the appropriate content action

Depending on the page, the appropriate action may be to refresh, improve, merge, prune, or continue monitoring it.

Priority 5 — Monitor after any change

Track subsequent performance rather than assuming that a change caused an improvement.
